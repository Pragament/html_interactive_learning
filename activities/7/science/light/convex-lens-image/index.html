1. Visual Lens Geometry & Physical Curvature

A convex lens (biconvex lens) is bounded by two spherical surfaces bulging outward. In our virtual laboratory, adjusting the Lens Thickness slider visually alters the physical cross-sectional profile and curvature of the glass:

Key Optical Fact: Thin-lens equations assume that the axial thickness (t) is negligible compared to the radii of curvature (R1) and (R2) ((t ll R1, R2)). In the thin-lens approximation, the lens is treated as a single refracting plane located at the optical centre O.

2. The Lens Maker's Formula for Real Lenses

In rigorous optics, the optical power (P = 1/f) of a thick biconvex lens in air (refractive index (n)) is governed by the full Lens Maker's Equation:

1 / f = (n − 1) • [ 1/R₁ − 1/R₂ + ((n − 1)•t) / (n • R₁ • R₂) ]

Notice that the physical thickness t introduces a thickness-dependent correction to the lens power:

  • The two principal planes (H1) and (H2) shift further inward from the vertices.
  • For identical surface radii of curvature, increased thickness slightly reduces the back focal distance.
  • Thicker or strongly curved lenses can exhibit increased spherical aberration, while chromatic aberration primarily arises from the wavelength dependence of the refractive index (dispersion).

3. Cartesian Sign Convention Summary

This virtual laboratory adheres strictly to the internationally standardized Cartesian Sign Convention:

  • All distances are measured from the Optical Centre (O) as the origin ((0, 0)).
  • Distances measured in the direction of incident light (to the right of O) are taken as Positive (+).
  • Distances measured against the direction of incident light (to the left of O) are taken as Negative (−).
  • Heights measured vertically above the principal axis are Positive (+); heights measured below are Negative (−).
  • Hence, for a real object placed on the left: (u < 0). For a convex lens: (f > 0).
