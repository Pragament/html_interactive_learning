Verify Laws of Reflection

First Law of Reflection — Same Plane
1. Same Plane Principle

"The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane."

💡 Class VI Language: Think of drawing on a flat desk. All three lines (incoming ray, normal, outgoing ray) rest completely flat on that exact same sheet!
Second Law of Reflection — Equal Angles
2. Equal Angles Principle

"The angle of incidence (i) is equal to the angle of reflection (r). Both angles are measured from the normal."

θᵢ = θᵣ
Angle of Incidence (i) = Angle of Reflection (r)
💡 Class VI Rule: If light strikes at 30° from the normal line, it bounces away at exactly 30° from the normal line (i = r)!

📖 Learn the Concept (Class VI Guide)

Fundamental laws and key scientific terms for Class VI Science (Natural Phenomena: Reflection of Light):

Core Principle 1
First Law of Reflection: Same Plane

"The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane."

Core Principle 2
Second Law of Reflection: Equal Angles

"The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection."
Formula: i = r (or θᵢ = θᵣ).

Plane of Incidence
The geometrical flat plane containing the incident ray and the normal at the point of incidence (represented by the flat drawing sheet).
Normal Line (N)
An imaginary line drawn perpendicular (at a right angle, 90°) to the mirror surface at the exact point of incidence.
Angle of Incidence (i)
The angle between the incoming incident ray and the normal line (never measured from the mirror surface).
Angle of Reflection (r)
The angle between the bounced reflected ray and the normal line (always equal to i).
Incident Ray
The incoming beam of light traveling from the light source towards the mirror surface.
Reflected Ray
The outgoing beam of light bouncing off the mirror surface back into the same optical medium.
Point of Incidence (O)
The exact point on the mirror surface where the incident ray strikes.
Plane Mirror
A flat, smooth reflecting surface coated with reflective silver backing on one side.
Why Knitting Needles / Pins?
Needles mark physical points along the ray paths so students can join pinholes with a pencil and ruler to measure angles accurately with a protractor.
