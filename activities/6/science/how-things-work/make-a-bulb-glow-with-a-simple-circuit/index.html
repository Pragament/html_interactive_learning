Step 0 · Identify the pins before you plug anything in

A 4-pin RGB LED has one common lead plus three colour leads. Nothing is printed on the package and the lead order changes between manufacturers, so you must test it. It takes about a minute — and it saves you from wiring the whole breadboard twice.

Tap any lead to inspect it.

  1. 1 Find the longest lead. That is the common pin — the only lead that is shared by all three colours. In a common-cathode LED it is the cathode (−); in a common-anode LED it is the anode (+).
  2. 2 Note which side the flat edge is on. The flat on the rim of the package tells you which way round the part is, but the colour order after it varies by manufacturer — never trust it on its own.
  3. 3 Test the other three leads. Use a multimeter in diode-test mode, or a 3 V coin cell + 220 Ω resistor. Whichever lead makes the LED glow is that colour.

Student → Teacher : Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know which lead is which on my RGB LED? Nothing is labelled.
Three steps, in order:

1 · Length. The longest lead is the common pin — cathode in a common-cathode part, anode in a common-anode part.
2 · Flat edge. Find the flat on the rim of the package. It orients the part, but the colour order after it is not standardised.
3 · Test. Multimeter in diode-test mode (or 3 V coin cell + 220 Ω):
  • Common cathode → red probe (+) on a colour lead, black probe (−) on the longest lead.
  • Common anode → black probe (−) on a colour lead, red probe (+) on the longest lead.
Whichever lead lights up tells you that colour. If nothing lights in either arrangement, the LED may be dead (or your supply is below its forward voltage — very common with blue).
Why does current flow from the + rail, through the resistor, then the LED — and not the other way round?
"Conventional current" is defined as flowing from + to −. In a common-cathode setup the + rail sits at 3 V and the common cathode sits at 0 V, so charge is pushed out of the + rail, down through the resistor, through the LED (anode → cathode), and out to GND. If you flip the LED around it becomes reverse biased — the junction blocks, current stops, and nothing lights.
Does it matter whether the resistor is before or after the LED?
No. A series loop has the same current at every point. The resistor's only job is to limit that current. What does matter is the polarity across the LED. (In common-anode wiring the resistor actually ends up after the LED, and it works exactly the same.)
Why does each colour need its own resistor? Can't I share one?
Because each colour has a different forward voltage and a different brightness per milliamp. If you share one resistor, the three LEDs fight over the current — one lights brightly, another is dim, and the shared value is wrong for all of them. One resistor per colour, always.
How do I choose the resistor value?
Use R = (V_supply − V_f) ÷ I.
Example — red, Vf ≈ 1.8 V, target 10 mA on a 3 V supply:
R = (3.0 − 1.8) / 0.010 = 120 Ω
Then round up to the next standard value (E12 series: 100, 120, 150, 180 …).
Why is my blue LED so dim compared with red and green?
Blue LEDs have the highest forward voltage — roughly 2.6 V to 3.2 V. With only 3.0 V from two cells there is almost nothing left to drop across the resistor, so the current is tiny. Many real blue LEDs won't light at all from 2 × AA. Try 3 cells (4.5 V) or a lower-value resistor.
Where does 3 V come from if each cell is only 1.5 V?
The cells are in series: the + terminal of one touches the − terminal of the other, so the voltages add. 1.5 V + 1.5 V = 3.0 V. (In parallel they'd stay 1.5 V but last twice as long.)
Do I need a resistor on the common pin?
No. In common cathode the common pin is the cathode (0 V) — the resistors go on the anode side, one per colour. In common anode the common pin is +3 V and the resistors go on the cathode side. Either way: one resistor per colour, never on the common.
My LED doesn't light at all. What should I check?
In order: 1) Is that colour's switch closed? 2) Is the resistor too large (too little current)? 3) Is the LED the right way round — longest leg on the correct rail? 4) Is the common pin actually on the correct rail (+3 V for common anode, GND for common cathode)? 5) Is the LED sitting across the centre channel of the breadboard?
Is the current "used up" as it goes around the circuit?
No! Current is the same everywhere in a series loop. What gets "used up" is voltage. The resistor drops VR, the LED drops Vf, and those two add up to the supply: V_R + V_f = 3.0 V.
Why do the dots move faster when I lower the resistance?
Lower resistance → more current. Current is literally "charge per second", so more charge passes each point per second — the animation shows this as dots moving faster and getting slightly larger.
What happens if I close two switches at once?
Each branch is independent, so both LEDs light and the light mixes additively: red + green = yellow, red + blue = magenta, green + blue = cyan, all three = white-ish. Notice that the common pin carries the sum of the branch currents.
Why does the current have to go all the way back to the battery?
Because it's a closed loop. Charge that leaves the + terminal must return to the − terminal. If the path is broken anywhere (open switch, missing jumper), no charge can move anywhere in that loop — which is exactly why opening one switch instantly stops that colour's dots.
What's the difference between common cathode and common anode?
Only which pin is shared. Common cathode: the three cathodes are joined internally and go to GND; you drive each anode through its own resistor. Common anode: the three anodes are joined and go to +3 V; you pull each cathode down through its own resistor. The current through each LED still flows anode → cathode in both cases.
