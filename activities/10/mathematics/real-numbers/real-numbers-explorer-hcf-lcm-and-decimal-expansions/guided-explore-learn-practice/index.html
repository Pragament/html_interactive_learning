What is prime factorisation?

Prime number: exactly two factors — 1 and itself (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, …). Composite number: more than two factors (4, 6, 8, 9, 10, …). 1 is neither.

Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: every composite number can be written as a product of primes in exactly one way, apart from the order of the factors.

Worked example — factorise 36

Step 1: 36 = 4 × 9
Step 2: 4 = 2 × 2, and 9 = 3 × 3
Step 3: 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3²

Check: 2² × 3² = 4 × 9 = 36 ✓

Finding HCF and LCM from prime factors

HCF = product of the lowest power of every prime common to both numbers.
LCM = product of the highest power of every prime that appears in either number.

Example: 12 = 2² × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3².

  • HCF = 2¹ × 3¹ = 6 (lowest shared powers)
  • LCM = 2² × 3² = 36 (highest powers overall)

Useful identity (two numbers only): HCF(a, b) × LCM(a, b) = a × b. Check: 6 × 36 = 216 = 12 × 18 ✓. This identity does not extend to three or more numbers.

Common mistakes

  • Treating 1 as a prime number — it isn't.
  • Stopping a split too early, e.g. writing 4 instead of 2 × 2.
  • For LCM, forgetting a prime that appears in only one of the numbers.
  • Mixing up HCF (smallest shared power) with LCM (largest overall power).
  • Applying HCF × LCM = product to three or more numbers — it only holds for exactly two.
